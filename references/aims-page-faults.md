# Common Specific Aims page faults

A checklist for reviewer agents and for the synthesis step. Each fault lists what it looks like and which factor it hits. Study-section-specific fault lines live in that section's panel.md.

## Structure and framing (Factor 1)

1. No clear gap. The opening paragraph describes a disease burden and a field but never states what is unknown. Reviewers cannot write "this addresses a critical gap" if the page never names one.
2. Gap is not the one the aims fill. The page names one gap, then the aims address something adjacent.
3. Burden-to-biology leap. Paragraph one is about patients (how many people have CKD); the aims are about a protein in a cell line, with no stated bridge.
4. Hypothesis is missing, vague or untestable. "We hypothesize that X plays a role in Y." Reviewers want direction and mechanism: X does what, through what, measured how.
5. Hypothesis is circular or already established. Preliminary data already proves it, so the aims are confirmatory.
6. Innovation is asserted, not shown. "Highly innovative" with no statement of what is new relative to prior work. Or innovation is only technical (a new method) with no conceptual payoff.
7. Payoff paragraph is generic. "Will lead to new therapies" without saying what will be known at the end that is not known now, and what that changes.
8. Wrong panel. The framing matches a different study section's scope better (see panel.md boundaries). Risk: a room that weighs the work differently, or reassignment.

## Aims design (Factor 2 red flags visible on the aims page)

9. Interdependent aims. Aim 2 needs Aim 1 to succeed. Reviewers ask, "if Aim 1 fails, what is left?"
10. Descriptive aim with no perturbation. Atlas, profiling or "characterize" aims without a test of function.
11. Correlation presented as mechanism. "X is upregulated in disease, therefore X drives disease."
12. Single model. One mouse line, one Cre driver, one injury model, or one cell line carries the whole claim.
13. Model mismatch. The model does not reproduce the human feature the significance paragraph emphasizes (e.g., ARPKD claims in an ADPKD model; chronic rejection claims in an acute model; uremia claims in non-uremic animals).
14. Readout mismatch. The claim is functional (transport, channel activity, GFR, nerve activity) but the readout is a marker (western blot, immunostaining, transcript).
15. Scope too big. Three aims each worth an R01; too many models, cohorts or techniques for the budget and years. Reviewers call this "overly ambitious."
16. Scope too small. Incremental; one experiment stretched into three aims.
17. Sex and other biological variables absent. No mention of both sexes, age or developmental stage where they obviously matter.
18. Human relevance absent. Purely rodent, no human tissue, cohort or data where a panel with clinicians will expect it.
19. Pharmacology without genetics (or genetics without rescue). Inhibitors with known off-target effects carry a causal claim alone.
20. Clinical aims without endpoint, power or target-engagement signals. Primary outcome not named; effect size not plausible; no evidence the intervention hits its target.
21. Biomarker aims without validation. No independent validation cohort, no reference standard, diagnosis confused with prediction.
22. Premise problems. The rationale rests on a contested or refuted finding without acknowledging the contrary evidence.

## Expertise signals (Factor 3)

23. Method without person. A specialized method (micropuncture, ciliary patch clamp, snRNA-seq analysis, large-animal surgery, clinical trial management) with no named collaborator or prior track record.
24. Early-stage investigator overreach. An ESI page proposing a program that reads like a center grant.

## Writing and readability (all reviewers, especially unassigned)

25. Jargon wall. Acronyms and gene names stacked so that an outsider in the room cannot follow. Most voters are outsiders to the subfield.
26. Wall of text. No visual hierarchy, no bolded hypothesis or aim titles, no white space. Reviewers skim.
27. Figure that does not earn its space, or an aims figure too small to read.
28. Aim titles that are activities, not outcomes. "Aim 1: To characterize..." versus "Aim 1: Determine whether X is required for Y."
29. Over-hedging or over-claiming. "May possibly contribute" or "will cure."
30. Preliminary data mentioned but not stated. "Our preliminary data support this" without saying what was found.

## How to use this list

Reviewer agents: cite faults by number only when they apply, and quote the sentence from the aims page that shows the fault. Do not list faults that are not present.

Synthesis: a fault raised independently by two or more reviewers (especially from different clusters) is a "common fault" and goes at the top of the revision list.
