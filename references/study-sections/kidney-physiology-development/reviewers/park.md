## Research focus
Park asks how multipotent nephron progenitor cells decide their fates and give rise to the many distinct cell types of the nephron. His stated aims are to define the roles of developmental signaling pathways (Wnt, Notch, Tgf/Bmp, Fgf, Hedgehog, retinoic acid) in nephron formation, to identify the transcription factors that establish each segment's identity, and to work out how those factors and pathways combine in gene regulatory networks. Landmark results include: Six2 and beta-catenin sharing target genes to balance progenitor renewal against commitment; Notch acting by downregulating Six2 and being needed for formation of all nephron segments rather than specifying proximal fate; beta-catenin being required for several segments along the proximal-distal axis; Hnf4a being required for proximal tubule maturation, with its loss reproducing Fanconi renotubular syndrome; Cdh6-expressing cells acting as proximal tubule progenitors; and proximal tubule cells giving rise to the thin descending limb of the loop of Henle. Recent work extends into the mature kidney, asking whether HNF4A and HNF1B are needed to maintain proximal tubule identity after differentiation, and whether loss of these factors unmasks segment plasticity and injury-associated states relevant to maladaptive repair.

## Model systems and methods
- Mouse genetics with segment- and lineage-specific Cre drivers (Six2-Cre/Six2GFPCre, Wnt4-Cre, Hoxb7-Cre, Slc34a1-Cre for proximal tubule, a distal nephron Cre for DCT/CNT, Foxd1-Cre for stroma); conditional deletion of Hnf4a, Hnf1b, Smo, Shh, beta-catenin, Notch pathway components, and Tcf21
- Knock-in alleles built in-house, including a Cre-inducible Yap gain-of-function allele and a knock-in Six2 Cre that avoids the mosaicism and altered progenitor dynamics of the widely used transgenic line
- Lineage tracing with fluorescent reporters; genetic gain-of-function (stabilized beta-catenin, NICD, active YAP)
- Single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-seq, scATAC-seq, RNA velocity, integration of multiple public developing-kidney datasets
- Genome-wide transcription factor binding: ChIP-seq (Six2, beta-catenin, Hoxd11, Osr1, Wt1, CTCF), CUT&RUN, segment-specific translating/nuclear transcriptional profiling
- Cis-regulatory analysis: enhancer deletion in mice (Six2 distal enhancer, Wnt4 enhancer), 4C-seq, insulator/boundary analysis
- Immunofluorescence and confocal imaging of embryonic and postnatal kidney; apicobasal polarity and junction markers; physiological phenotyping of mutants (polyuria, glycosuria, phosphaturia, nephrocalcinosis, BUN/creatinine)
- Collaborations extending the toolkit to human kidney spatial transcriptomics, zebrafish vascular genetics, bladder/urothelium development, and intestinal and cancer transcription networks

## Likely reviewing lens (inferred)
- **Will value:** genetic rigor. He builds his own alleles and Cre lines, and his 2026 preprint on a knock-in Six2 line is an explicit argument that a widely used transgenic driver is mosaic and alters progenitor dynamics. Expect him to reward applications that justify driver choice, show recombination efficiency and specificity, and include proper lineage tracing controls. He also values pairing transcriptomic data with direct binding data (ChIP-seq or CUT&RUN) and with functional enhancer or gene deletion, rather than correlation alone. Integration of multiple single-cell datasets and careful cluster annotation using marker genes is standard practice in his papers.
- **Will scrutinize hardest:** (1) Cre-driver specificity and timing, especially claims about one segment when the driver also labels neighbors; (2) "transcription factor X regulates gene Y" asserted from expression change without binding or a deleted regulatory element; (3) cell-fate and plasticity claims from scRNA-seq clustering without lineage tracing (he has shown both that proximal tubule cells make the thin descending limb and that progenitors can transiently contribute to interstitium, and he will ask how the applicant distinguishes transdifferentiation from a shared origin); (4) non-cell-autonomous effects being read as cell-autonomous, a distinction he explicitly makes in the YAP and Tcf21 papers; (5) whether a developmental phenotype has functional or physiological consequence (he backs his mouse models with urine and serum phenotyping); (6) organoid-based claims measured against in vivo segment identity, given the known gap in distal and stromal representation.
- **Where he is an outsider:** clinical nephrology, transplantation and immunology, hemodynamics and micropuncture-style transport physiology, epidemiology and trials, and dialysis. He has no clinical appointment. He is competent but not a specialist in channel biophysics and whole-animal electrolyte balance, though his mutants show transport phenotypes.
- **Likely questions at the table:** "Which Cre is this, and how specific and efficient is it at the stage you claim?" "Is the effect cell-autonomous?" "Do you have binding data for the direct targets, or only differential expression?" "Have you considered that these cells share a developmental origin rather than converting?" "Does the mutant have a functional phenotype, or only a marker change?" "How will you distinguish a genuine injury state from an immature or developmental program being reactivated?"
