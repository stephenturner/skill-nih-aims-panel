## 1. The 1-9 scale

Whole numbers only. Lower is better. 5 is "good, medium impact" and is the average. 1 and 9 should be rare. Reviewers are told to use the whole range and not to compress scores into the top half.

| Score | Descriptor | Range | Typical pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Exceptional | High | Exceptionally strong, essentially no weaknesses |
| 2 | Outstanding | High | Extremely strong, negligible weaknesses |
| 3 | Excellent | High | Very strong, only some minor weaknesses |
| 4 | Very Good | Medium | Strong, but numerous minor weaknesses |
| 5 | Good | Medium | Strong, but at least one moderate weakness |
| 6 | Satisfactory | Medium | Some strengths, some moderate weaknesses |
| 7 | Fair | Low | Some strengths, at least one major weakness |
| 8 | Marginal | Low | A few strengths, a few major weaknesses |
| 9 | Poor | Low | Very few strengths, numerous major weaknesses |

(The "typical pattern" column is NIH's long-standing companion guidance on minor/moderate/major weaknesses; use it to keep scores consistent with the critique text.)

Final impact score = mean of all eligible members' scores x 10, so the range is 10-90. In practice, scores in the 10s-20s are in the funding range at most institutes for R01s, 30s are borderline, and above ~40 is unlikely to be funded. Paylines vary by institute and year; never promise a percentile.

## 2. Simplified Review Framework

Applies to R01, R03, R15, R16, R21, R33, R34, R61, U01, UG3/UH3 and other research project grants with due dates on or after January 25, 2025. The five regulatory criteria are reorganized into three factors.

### Factor 1. Importance of the Research (Significance + Innovation). Scored 1-9.
- Significance: importance relative to current scientific challenges and opportunities; whether it addresses an important gap, solves a critical problem, or creates a valuable conceptual or technical advance. Also the rationale and the rigor of the scientific background (prior literature, preliminary data) and whether that background justifies the study.
- Innovation: the extent to which novelty affects importance. A project that is not novel can still be critical to the field. Novel concepts, methods or technologies, or existing ones used in new ways.

### Factor 2. Rigor and Feasibility (Approach). Scored 1-9.
- Rigor: unbiased, reproducible, robust data; experimental design and controls; sample size justification; analysis, interpretation and reporting plans; relevant biological variables such as sex and age. For human or animal work also: rigor of the intervention, justified outcome variables, generalizability (or relevance to a rare subgroup), appropriate and sufficiently diverse sample. Inclusion plans for human subjects.
- Feasibility: whether the approach is sound and achievable, including plans for problems that arise. Where feasibility is less certain, whether the uncertainty is balanced by potential for major advances. For human studies, recruitment and retention feasibility. For clinical trials, timeline and milestones.

### Factor 3. Expertise and Resources (Investigators + Environment). Not scored.
- Rated "Appropriate" or "Additional expertise and/or resources needed" (gaps must be explained).
- Investigators: background, training and expertise appropriate to career stage for the proposed work; leadership plan quality for multi-PI.
- Environment: institutional resources appropriate for the work.
- The binary rating was introduced to stop general reputation from inflating scores. Reviewers judge fit to the work, not fame.

## 3. How factors combine into Overall Impact

Overall Impact is the likelihood the project will exert a sustained, powerful influence on the field. It is not an average of factor scores.

The SRF scoring guidance sets a hierarchy:
- Factor 1 sets the ceiling. "A project of moderate or limited importance (Factor 1) cannot be made more impactful by a strong approach (Factor 2) and/or appropriate expertise and resources (Factor 3)." If Factor 1 is a 5, Overall Impact is 5 or worse.
- Factors 2 and 3 can hold the score at that ceiling or pull it down. Example from the guidance: important research (Factor 1 = 2) with a moderate approach or gaps in expertise can end up anywhere from 3 to 9.
- Concerns under Additional Review Criteria can only push toward a worse score.
- For Factors 1 and 2, high scores must rest on strengths, not on the absence of weaknesses. Low scores must rest on named weaknesses.

Practical consequence for aims pages: the aims page is where Factor 1 is won or lost. Unassigned reviewers form their Factor 1 view from it, and Factor 1 caps everything else.

## 4. Additional review criteria and considerations

Affect the overall score (no separate score): human subjects protections, vertebrate animals, biohazards, resubmission (judge as now presented), renewal (progress), revision (scope).

Considered but do not affect score: authentication of key biological/chemical resources; budget and period of support.

No longer evaluated by reviewers: foreign organizations, select agents, resource sharing plans (NIH staff handle these). Inclusion and clinical trial timelines moved into Factor 2.

## 5. How a study section meeting works

- Each application gets (usually) three assigned reviewers: R1 (primary), R2 (secondary), R3 (reader/discussant). They write full critiques and give preliminary Factor 1, Factor 2 and Overall Impact scores, plus the Factor 3 rating.
- Applications in roughly the bottom half by preliminary score are usually Not Discussed (ND). ND applications receive the written critiques and criterion scores but no impact score. Any member can ask to discuss an ND application.
- For discussed applications: assigned reviewers state preliminary scores, R1 presents, R2 and R3 add points of agreement or disagreement, the floor opens, then the assigned reviewers state final scores. This creates a "range." Every eligible member then votes privately; voting outside the range is allowed but should be announced.
- Unassigned members vote on every discussed application. Many read only the abstract, the Specific Aims page and the critiques before the meeting. That is why the aims page has to carry the significance argument by itself and has to be legible to someone outside the subfield.
- The Chair runs discussion and summarizes but also votes. The SRO manages the meeting and writes the resume of discussion; the SRO does not score.
- Reviewers must score the application as written. They cannot score it assuming a weak aim will be dropped.

## 6. What can and cannot be judged from an aims page alone

Reviewer agents work only from the aims page, so they should be explicit about this.

Can judge well:
- Factor 1 in full: the gap, why it matters, the central hypothesis, novelty, the payoff.
- Logic and structure: whether aims test the hypothesis, whether aims depend on each other, whether the scope fits the mechanism and timeline.
- Red flags for Factor 2: premise problems, unrealistic scope, untestable hypotheses, missing controls that the design implies, model choices that cannot answer the question, no mention of sex as a variable, single-model reliance, descriptive aims with no perturbation.
- Signals for Factor 3: whether the named team covers the methods (e.g., micropuncture proposed with no one who does it).

Cannot judge (say what you would look for in the Research Strategy instead of assuming):
- Power calculations, detailed statistical plans, full preliminary data, alternative strategies, timelines, letters of support, biosketch depth.

Score Factor 2 and Overall Impact as "provisional, based on the aims page," and list what the Research Strategy must show to keep that score.

## 7. Mechanism differences

- R01: full research program; preliminary data expected; 4-5 years; reviewers expect interlocking aims with a coherent hypothesis and a credible path to impact.
- R21: exploratory/developmental; preliminary data not required; reviewers should reward high-risk, high-payoff ideas and should not demand R01-level feasibility. Two aims is normal. Panels still sometimes penalize R21s for lack of feasibility data; flag it but note the guidance.
- R03: small, often a single aim or a pilot.
- K awards (K01, K08, K23, K99/R00) and F fellowships are not under the RPG Simplified Review Framework. They are scored on candidate, career development plan, mentors, research plan and environment. If the aims page is for a K or F, have reviewers score the research plan in that context (training value, fit to the candidate, feasibility in the award period) and note that candidate and mentoring criteria cannot be judged from the aims page.
- Clinical trial applications: inclusion, recruitment, timeline and milestones sit in Factor 2.
- Resubmissions (A1): the aims page should reflect the changes; reviewers judge the application as now presented but will notice whether prior critiques were addressed.
